# Site Reliability Engineering (SRE) — Real Interview Questions

> ~60 questions across 5 parts. SRE interviews grade: whether you can *quantify* reliability (SLOs, error budgets), operate an incident calmly, and balance reliability vs velocity. The number one tell: can you do the *math* on an SLO?

---

## Part 1 — Basic Level (phone screen / fundamentals)

**Q1. SLI, SLO, SLA — define each and give one of each.**
→ **SLI** = a *measurement* of a service (a percentage): "99.9% of requests return 200 in < 400ms." **SLO** = the *target* on an SLI: "we aim for 99.9%." **SLA** = the *contract* with a penalty if you miss it (business/legal, usually stricter or tied to the SLO). The classic error: calling an SLA an SLO. SLI is data, SLO is a goal, SLA is a promise with money on it.

**Q2. What's an error budget? How is it used?**
→ Error budget = 100% − SLO. For 99.9% (monthly), that's ~43.2 min of "allowed" downtime. It's the *currency* that trades reliability for velocity: budget available → ship features fast; budget nearly spent → freeze risky changes, invest in reliability. "The error budget is the only objective number that ends the 'ship it' vs 'it's not ready' argument."

**Q3. Monitoring vs observability. What's the difference?**
→ Monitoring = "is the thing I *expected* healthy?" (dashboards of known metrics, alerts on thresholds). Observability = "can I ask *new* questions about why it's unhealthy, without changing the code?" (metrics + logs + traces, with enough cardinality to explore). "Monitoring tells you *that* it's broken; observability tells you *why*."

**Q4. The three pillars. What does each actually catch?**
→ **Metrics** (prometheus/CloudWatch): trends, rates, saturation — *when* something changed. **Logs** (structured, queryable): the *specific* error and context (the exception, the request). **Traces** (distributed): the *path* of one slow request across services (which hop was slow). "Metrics for the alarm, logs for the evidence, traces for the *where*."

**Q5. What's a good alert? The criteria.**
→ (1) **Actionable** (a human can do something *right now* — not "investigate"). (2) **Signal > noise** (pages only when SLO/user-impacting, not on every wiggle). (3) **Specific** (which service, which region, what broke). (4) **Has a runbook** (the alert links to the *first three things to do*). The anti-pattern it kills: "CPU > 80" that pages 20x a week until it's muted.

**Q6. What is toil? Give an example and how you'd kill it.**
→ Manual, repetitive, operationally taxing, *unnecessary* work that doesn't scale with the product (hand-provisioning, copy-pasting config, re-running a failed deploy by hand). Example: "every new service needs a dashboard someone hand-builds" → kill it with a golden-template dashboard that auto-generates from metadata. "Toil is the tax on being a human; SRE's job is to make it → 0 (Google's target is < 50% of time on toil)."

**Q7. What's a postmortem? Blameless — what does that actually mean?**
→ A written, time-ordered account of an incident: timeline, impact (numbers), root cause(s), what went well, what went wrong, and **action items with owners + due dates**. Blameless = we analyze the *system* that allowed it (the process, the gap), not punish the person — because the person who paged will page again, and we want *honest* timelines. "Blameless isn't 'no consequences' — it's 'consequences for the system, and the fix has a name and a date.'"

**Q8. What's MTTR, MTBF, MTTD? Which do you actually optimize?**
→ **MTTD** = mean time to *detect*. **MTTR** = mean time to *recover* (detection → restored). **MTBF** = mean time between failures. You optimize **MTTR** (get to green fast — rollback is the fastest MTTR) and **MTTD** (detect in seconds, not via a customer). MTBF is a *consequence*, not a target (chasing "fewer failures" often means shipping less).

**Q9. Explain a circuit breaker.**
→ A guard on a dependency call: after N failures (the "open" threshold), it *stops calling* and fails fast (returns the fallback immediately) for a cooldown, then "half-opens" (lets a few probes through) to test recovery. Why: a sick dependency otherwise *consumes* your capacity (timeouts pile up, threads/queues saturate, you die *with* it). "The breaker is how you refuse to die of a heart attack when your kidney fails."

**Q10. What's the difference between a warning and a page? Who gets paged?**
→ **Page** = "act now, at 3am, user/business impact" (an SLO breach or a fast-burning failure). **Warning/ticket** = "needs attention during the day" (a trend, headroom, a non-user-facing metric). Paged by a *human* (a rotation) who owns it. The discipline: pages are *rare* by design — if you page daily, you've built a warning that's shouting, and it will be muted (and then it'll miss the real one).

**Q11. What's capacity planning? How do you do it for a web service?**
→ Measure the *per-unit* cost (CPU/mem per request, per RPS), find the *ceiling* (load-test to the limit), and set a *target utilization* (e.g., 60% at peak — headroom for spikes). Then: autoscale to hold that target, and plan the *growth* (when does 1M RPS hit the ceiling? what's the cost of 2x?). "Capacity isn't a number you pick — it's the RPS where latency starts to bend, and the headroom you buy above it."

**Q12. What is a runbook? What makes a *good* one?**
→ A step-by-step "when X happens, do Y" for an alert: the symptom, the first 3 diagnostic commands, the *safe* mitigation (rollback/scale/flag), the escalation path, and the comms template. Good = **tested** (you've actually run it), **short** (the 3am-you is tired), and **owned** (someone reviews it quarterly — an untested runbook is a hope).

**Q13. What's the 12-factor rule for reliability (roughly)? Pick the 3 that matter most.**
→ (Stateless app — scale by cloning; config in env not code; dispose via signal; logs to stdout; etc.) The 3 that matter most for reliability: **stateless** (a node can die and be replaced with zero data loss — the basis of auto-healing), **config as env** (you can flip behavior without redeploy — the basis of fast rollback/feature-flags), and **logs to stdout** (centralized, queryable — the basis of observability).

**Q14. What is a synthetic monitor?**
→ A *scripted* check that runs from outside the system (every 30s, from multiple regions) hitting the critical user path ("can a customer in Frankfurt buy a thing?"). It catches failures *before* users do (DNS, TLS, a 500 on the checkout path) and it's *independent* of your internal dashboards (which might be the thing that's down). "Synthetics are the customer's eyes on your SLA."

**Q15. What's the difference between latency p50 and p99? Why does p99 matter?**
→ p50 = the *median* (half of requests are this fast). p99 = 99% of requests are this fast (the *tail*). p99 matters because: (1) it's the *real user* experience (the 1% who wait 10s are your angry tickets), (2) tail latency is where *dependency* problems show (one slow downstream call ruins the p99 even if the p50 is fine), (3) SLOs are usually on the p99, not the p50. "p50 is the average customer; p99 is the customer who files the ticket."

## Part 2 — Real Job-Specific Questions (the SRE day job)

**Q16. How do you set an SLO for a new service? Walk it through.**
→ (1) Pick the *user-facing* SLI (not "CPU" — "success rate of the core action": a checkout, a search that returns results). (2) Baseline *current* behavior (you can't target what you haven't measured — run 2 weeks). (3) Set the SLO *with the product* (what does "good" mean to the user? what's the cost of being wrong?). (4) Start slightly *above* current (a stretch, not a fantasy) and add a *multi-window, burn-rate* alert. (5) Review monthly (is the budget right? is the SLO too easy/hard?). "An SLO set in a vacuum is a number nobody trusts — it has to be *agreed* and *baselined*."

**Q17. Design the alerting for a service with an SLO. (Burn rate — go deep.)**
→ Multi-window burn-rate alerts: "the error budget is burning at X% over a 1h window AND a 6h window." The two windows: **fast burn** (14.4x the budget in 1h → page in ~1h — a real incident now) and **slow burn** (1x over 6h → ticket — a leak, not an emergency). Why two: a single window either pages on every blip (1h only) or misses fast incidents (6h only). "The burn rate converts 'we're having a bad day' into 'we'll breach the SLO in N hours if we do nothing' — that's the only number worth paging on."

**Q18. On-call: how do you design a rotation that doesn't burn people out?**
→ Small (2–4 people), short (1 week max, or 1 week + a follow-week off), with a **follow-up** (the week after your on-call you're *off* — you're drained). A *handoff doc* (open incidents, watch-items, what changed this week). A **page budget** (if pages > N/week, it's a *reliability* problem, not an on-call problem — stop and fix the alerts). And: on-call is *compensated/recognized* and *rotates fairly* (no one is a permanent on-call). "A sustainable rotation is a *reliability metric* — burnout is a leading indicator of the next missed incident."

**Q19. How do you reduce alert fatigue? (You're getting 40 pages/week.)**
→ (1) **Triage every page**: for each, was it actionable? If not, it's a warning → demote it. (2) **Consolidate**: 5 alerts for "the DB is sick" → 1 (alert on the *SLO*, not the 5 sub-symptoms). (3) **Add the missing context** (a page with a link to the runbook + the 3 metrics stops people "investigating" for 20 min). (4) **Track a metric**: pages/week, % actionable, % muted — and the goal is to *cut* it (40 → < 10). (5) **The root cause**: 40 pages usually means you're alerting on *symptoms* (CPU, mem) instead of the *SLO* — fix the alerting model, not the thresholds. "Alert fatigue isn't a threshold problem — it's a *signal* problem. Fewer, better alerts beat more, precise ones."

**Q20. How do you do a load test that's actually useful?**
→ (1) **Representative traffic** (record real traffic patterns — the mix of endpoints, not just "hammer /home"). (2) **A model**: what's the expected peak (10x current? the launch traffic?) and the *target* (p99 < X at that RPS). (3) **Load to the limit** (find the ceiling — where latency bends, where error rate spikes) — not just "to the expected peak." (4) **Observe the whole system** (the bottleneck is usually a *dependency*: a DB, a rate-limited API, a connection pool — load-test with real dependencies or realistic fakes). (5) **Repeat** (regression: does the new version hold the same ceiling?). "A load test that stops at the expected peak tells you nothing about the ceiling — go *past* it."

**Q21. A service has 99.9% uptime but users are angry. What's wrong?**
→ **Uptime is the wrong SLI.** 99.9% available but *slow* (p99 = 8s) or *partially broken* (search works, checkout doesn't) = "up" but useless. The fix: the SLI must measure the *user's actual job* ("a successful checkout in < 2s"), not "the LB returns 200." "Uptime answers 'is it on?' — users care 'does it do what I need?' Pick the SLI that matches the user's job-to-be-done."

**Q22. How do you design for graceful degradation?**
→ Tier features by *criticality*: **core** (must work: login, the primary action) > **important** (should work: search) > **nice** (can die: recommendations, analytics). When degrading, shed from the bottom up (turn off nice → queue important → protect core). Implement with **feature flags** (flip off in seconds, not a deploy) and **fallbacks** (stale cache, a default, a "try again later" that's honest). "Degradation is a *plan* you build *before* the incident — not a decision you make at 3am. The question is 'what dies first,' and you answer it in peacetime."

**Q23. How do you handle a dependency that has no SLA (a free 3rd-party API)?**
→ Treat it as *untrusted by default*: (1) **Timeout + retry** (bounded, with backoff — don't hang on it). (2) **Circuit breaker** (stop calling when it's sick). (3) **A fallback** (cached data, a degraded response, or queue-and-try-later — so its failure isn't *your* failure). (4) **Rate-limit your egress** (its limits are not your capacity budget). (5) **Observe it** (a dashboard of *its* latency/errors, independent of yours). "No SLA means it's a *risk*, not a *dependency* — design so its worst day is your Tuesday."

**Q24. How do you measure the reliability of a *batch* job (no users, no SLA)?**
→ There's no user SLI, so define *job* SLOs: **completion rate** (% of runs that finish), **freshness** (the data is < N hours old — the real user of a batch is a *dashboard*, and "stale" is its "down"), **duration** (it finished by the deadline), and **correctness** (row counts / validation checks pass). Alert on *freshness* (the dashboard going stale) not on "the job errored" (a job that runs but produces bad data is the worst case). "A batch job's user is the thing that reads its output — define the SLO on *their* experience (fresh, correct data), not the job's exit code."

**Q25. How do you do database failover for RDS (multi-AZ)? What's the real RTO?**
→ Multi-AZ = a synchronous standby in another AZ; on primary failure, RDS *automatically* fails over (DNS flips to the standby). The real RTO: **~60–120s** of connection loss (the failover + the app reconnecting). But: (1) your *app* must handle the connection blip (connection pool with retry, a transient-error handler — otherwise the 120s is a cascade of 500s). (2) **Test it** (force a failover in staging, measure the *actual* time and the app's behavior). "The RDS failover is fast; the *app's* reaction to it is the slow part — that's where your real MTTR lives."

**Q26. What's a canary deployment, and how does SRE gate it?**
→ Deploy the new version to a *small % of traffic* (1–5%) first, watch its metrics vs the baseline (canary analysis: is its error rate / p99 statistically worse?), and only then promote to 100%. SRE's role: the **gate** — the canary auto-aborts if its SLO burn rate spikes (a rollback in minutes, not a full rollout's worth of damage). "A canary is a *hypothesis test*: 'version N is as healthy as N-1' — and you're allowed to reject it with data, not with a hunch."

**Q27. How do you observe a Kubernetes cluster for SRE purposes?**
→ The platform metrics (node CPU/mem, pod restarts, OOMKills, pending pods, HPA scaling) + the *workload* metrics (the SLOs of the services *in* it — the cluster being "green" is meaningless if the service is slow) + the *control plane* (API server latency, etcd health — a sick control plane is a cluster that can't heal). And the SRE-specific signals: **pod churn** (CrashLooping = a burning service), **node pressure** (evictions), and **HPA flapping** (scaling up/down = an unstable load or a mis-tuned policy). "K8s gives you a lot of metrics; the SRE question is 'which of them predict *user* impact?' — start from the SLO, not the dashboard."

**Q28. What's a status page, and what goes on it?**
→ A public (or internal) "what's happening" for users: **current** incidents (impact in user terms — "checkouts are slow in EU," not "p99 elevated"), **ongoing** status (investigating / mitigating / resolved), and **recent** history (the last N incidents, with dates). The discipline: it's *honest* (say "investigating" when you don't know), *timely* (update it in the incident, not after), and *actionable* (what should the user do — "retry in a few minutes"). "The status page is your *reputation* during an incident — a silent outage is worse than an admitted one."

**Q29. How do you do incident communication (internal + external) during an incident?**
→ **Internal**: an incident channel, a **commander** (owns comms + coordination, *not* the fixer — the fixer focuses), status updates every 15–30 min (who's affected, what we're doing, next update ETA), and a clear handoff to whoever's fixing. **External**: the status page (user-impact language), and for big ones, proactive (don't wait for users to ask). The rule: **communicate the *decision*, not the *noise*** — "we're rolling back" is a decision; "I'm looking at the logs" is noise. "The commander's job is that *everyone* (team + users) knows the same thing at the same time — that's what prevents the 5-people-doing-5-different-things chaos."

**Q30. A rollback takes 20 minutes. How do you get it to 2 minutes?**
→ (1) **Immutable releases** (each version is a tagged artifact — rollback = point at the previous tag, not "rebuild the old code"). (2) **Automate it** (a `rollback` command / a button — one action, not a 10-step dance). (3) **Decouple the schema** (the #1 rollback blocker is a *migration* that's not reversible — design migrations to be backward-compatible: add-don't-change, so a code rollback never needs a schema rollback). (4) **Blue-green** (the old version is *still running* on the green — rollback = flip the LB, ~seconds). "Rollback time is a *design* property. A 20-min rollback means you've coupled the deploy to something (a schema, a rebuild, a manual step) that should be decoupled."

## Part 3 — Advanced Level (senior / design / the SRE craft)

**Q31. Design an SLO for a payment service. Go deep (this is the one).**
→ **SLI**: "a successful *authorized* payment in < 2s" (success = 2xx *and* the PSP confirms capture; the tail = the p99). **SLO**: 99.95% over a 30-day window (payments are money — stricter than a web page; say *why*: a failed payment = direct revenue loss + a support ticket). **Error budget**: 99.95%/30d ≈ 21.6 min/month. **Alerts**: fast burn (budget gone in ~1h → page) + slow burn (leaking over days → ticket). **The hard part, said out loud**: the *denominator* (what counts as a "payment attempt" — retries? duplicates?) and the *PSP's* reliability (your SLO is bounded by the payment processor's — so you measure *your* contribution separately and have a PSP failover). "A payment SLO is the hardest kind because the failure is *money*, the SLI has a *tail* (the slow authorization), and your ceiling is a *third party's* uptime."

**Q32. How do you design for a 99.99% (four nines) service? What does it actually take?**
→ 99.99% = ~4.3 min/month. That's **not** a single region (a regional blip is 30+ min). It takes: (1) **Multi-region active-active** (traffic serves from 2+ regions; one region dies, the other holds — say the data-sync model that makes this possible: it's the hard part). (2) **Stateless everything** (any node/region can drop). (3) **The data tier** is the bottleneck (active-active DB is very hard — often you do active-passive *per shard* or accept a split). (4) **Automated failover** (no human in the 4-min budget — DNS + LB + DB all auto). (5) **Chaos-tested** (you've killed a region and it held). "Four nines isn't a target you *configure* — it's an architecture where *no single region or node is a single point of failure*, and the data model is the part that decides if it's real."

**Q33. Explain a distributed system's consistency options and when you'd pick each.**
→ **Strong/linearizable** (every read sees the latest write — e.g., a ZK-like store, a single-writer DB): for *money, inventory, auth* (a wrong read = real harm). **Eventual** (reads may lag — Cassandra/DynamoDB): for *feeds, profiles, analytics* (a 5s-stale read is fine). **The senior answer**: it's *per-data*, not per-system — the *balance* is strongly consistent, the *feed* is eventual. Say the cost: strong consistency = slower + more availability sacrifice (a partition forces a quorum to refuse); eventual = faster + more available but you must *design for stale reads* (idempotency, versioning). "Pick consistency *per piece of data* by the cost of being wrong — not by the cloud's default."

**Q34. Design a global rate-limiting + abuse system for an auth endpoint (login).**
→ The endpoint is the #1 brute-force target. **Per-identity** (the username, not just the IP — attackers rotate IPs): token bucket per username (e.g., 5/min, 20/hour) + **progressive delay** (each failed login adds a delay). **Per-IP** as a second layer (an IP can try 100 usernames). **The hard part**: the limit must be *distributed* (multiple auth nodes) → a shared counter (Redis) with an atomic increment, or a *local* counter + async (accept slight over-limit in exchange for no Redis on the hot path). Plus: **lockout** (too many → temporary block + alert), **honeypot** (a fake field bots fill), and **the signal** (a spike in failed logins = an attack, page the security channel, not just "the endpoint is slow"). "Rate-limiting auth is a *security* control, not a performance one — the unit is the *identity*, and the failure mode is a *breach*, not a 500."

**Q35. Your SLO is being breached by *one* noisy tenant (a big customer doing 50% of traffic). What do you do?**
→ This is a **fairness/capacity** problem, not a reliability bug. (1) **Quantify** (how much of the budget is *this* tenant burning? is it within their agreed quota?). (2) **Short term**: per-tenant rate limit / quota (cap the noisy tenant so it can't starve the others — you may need to *throttle a paying customer*, which is a business conversation, say that). (3) **Structural**: **isolation** (a separate queue/pool/shard for big tenants so they can't affect small ones — multi-tenancy with per-tenant capacity), and a **fair-scheduling** policy. (4) **The business angle**: a tenant that needs 50% of capacity is a *different product tier* — price it / architect it separately. "One tenant breaching the SLO is a *multi-tenancy design* gap — the fix is isolation + quota, not 'ask them to be nicer.'"

**Q36. How do you do capacity planning for a *spiky* workload (retail: Black Friday = 50x)?**
→ You don't run 50x capacity 365 days (it's 50x the cost for 1 day). The answer: (1) **Elastic** (autoscale to the spike — but test that it scales *fast enough*; a 50x spike in 10 min needs pre-warmed headroom). (2) **Pre-scale** (before the known event, raise the floor to N so the *first* 10 min is covered while autoscale catches up). (3) **Shed load** (the non-critical features are *off* for the event — recommendations, analytics — so the 50x hits the *core*, not everything). (4) **Queue the overflow** (a 51x request is *queued*, not *rejected* — "your order is in line" is better than "error"). (5) **The number**: plan for the *peak* you'll actually serve (you may *choose* to cap at 40x and degrade gracefully above it — that's a business decision, make it explicit). "Spike capacity is a *trade* between cost (50x always) and experience (degrade above N) — the SRE's job is to make that trade *explicit and tested*, not accidental."

**Q37. Design a chaos engineering program. (What, how, and the stopping criteria.)**
→ **What**: inject *controlled failures* (kill a node, cut a region's network, make the DB slow) in a *safe* way to *prove* the system survives its own assumptions. **How**: start small + local (kill one pod in staging → production), target *the assumptions* ("we assume the LB will fail over — does it?"), and measure *the SLO* during the fault (not "did it recover" but "did the *user* feel it"). **Stopping criteria** (non-negotiable): a *blast radius* (only 1 pod, only 5% of traffic), an *SLO guard* (if the error budget burns past X%, the experiment *aborts automatically* and rolls back), a *time box*, and a *go/no-go* with the team. "Chaos isn't 'break prod' — it's *falsify the assumption that the system is more reliable than it is*, with a hard stop that protects the SLO. The value is the *first* time you find out the failover doesn't work, being in a *Tuesday afternoon*, not a *Black Friday*."

**Q38. How do you design observability for a *serverless* (Lambda) system? (It's stateless + short-lived — what breaks?)**
→ What breaks: no long-lived node to "ssh into," a *cold start* that's invisible in the avg, and a *step* function (100 Lambdas, each 100ms, but the *request* is the unit). The design: (1) **Structured logs per invocation** (with a *correlation ID* that spans the whole request — S3 → Lambda → DynamoDB → the response, so you can follow *one* request across 4 stateless hops). (2) **Traces** (X-Ray — the *only* way to see the cross-Lambda path; the avg latency hides the slow invocation). (3) **The right metrics**: *invocation* error rate, *duration* p99 (and separate the *init* duration — a cold-start alert is its own thing), *throttle* count (you're hitting the concurrency limit = a capacity SLO), and *queue depth* upstream. (4) **The absence problem**: Lambda has no "uptime" — the SLI is the *request's* success, and "no invocations" (upstream broke) is as important as "invocations failing." "Serverless observability is *request-centric*, not *node-centric* — you observe the *flow*, not the *thing*, because there is no persistent thing."

**Q39. A service is "fine" on all metrics but *users* report it's broken 1% of the time. How do you find it?**
→ All metrics green + 1% of users broken = the *aggregation is hiding the tail*. (1) **Segment**: is it *one* segment? (a region, a device/OS version, a browser, a *tenant*, a specific *endpoint* — the 1% is usually a *cohort*, not random). (2) **The p99.9 / the errors that don't page**: look at the *error log* filtered to that cohort (the 1% is likely a *specific exception* that's below the alert threshold). (3) **The environment**: a *specific* path — an old mobile SDK, a *proxy* (a corporate firewall mangling the response), a *specific* data shape (a user with a *really long* name / a *unicode* edge case). (4) **The repro**: get a *request ID* from an affected user and *follow it* in the trace. "1% broken with green metrics is a *segmentation* problem — the average is lying. Find the cohort, get a request ID, follow the trace. It's almost never random."

**Q40. How do you design a *data* SLO (not an API SLO)?**
→ The "user" is a *downstream consumer* (a dashboard, a model, another service). The SLIs: **freshness** (the data is < N min old — the #1), **completeness** (all expected rows/records arrived — a 0.1% missing is a *wrong number* for finance), **correctness** (validation checks pass: ranges, uniqueness, reconciliation against the source), and **availability** (a query on the data returns in < X). Alert on *freshness* (stale = down) and *completeness* (a partial load is *worse* than no load — a dashboard showing 90% of the data *confidently* is the worst case). "A data SLO's failure mode is *silently wrong*, not *loudly down* — so the SLIs are about *rightness and freshness*, and the worst incident is the one where the number is wrong and nobody noticed for a week."

**Q41. Explain the "reliability is a feature" argument, and how you'd defend an SLO freeze to a PM who wants to ship now.**
→ Reliability *is* a feature: a checkout that works 99.9% of the time is a *better product* than one that works 99% — users *feel* the difference, and it's a *retention* metric. Defending the freeze: (1) **The number** (we've spent 80% of the error budget this month — the *data*, not an opinion). (2) **The trade made explicit** (the SLO says: "if we ship this *now*, we accept a higher chance the service is degraded for the next N days" — put *that* in front of the PM, let *them* own the decision, not you). (3) **The alternative** (ship *part* of it behind a flag at 10% — get the value *and* keep the budget). (4) **The reversal** (if the PM says "we accept the risk," *that's fine* — it's now a *business decision with the number on the table*, and the freeze is lifted *by them*, not fought). "The SRE doesn't say 'no' — we say 'here's the *price* in reliability, in minutes, and here's a way to pay it *partially*. The PM decides; we make the price *legible*."

**Q42. Design the *data path* for a real-time "system health" dashboard (aggregating 100 services).**
→ Ingest: each service emits metrics (prometheus/OTLP) → a *time-series* store (Prometheus/M3/TSDB) with *per-service* retention (hot 7d, cold 90d). Aggregate: a *query layer* that computes the SLOs (not raw metrics — the dashboard shows "99.97% of checkouts succeeded," not 40 graphs). **The hard parts**: (1) **Cardinality** (100 services × dimensions = a lot of series — cap labels, don't put user-IDs in metric labels). (2) **The "health" definition** (a service is "healthy" if its *SLO* is met — the dashboard is *SLO-centric*, not metric-centric). (3) **Latency** (the dashboard itself must be < 2s — pre-compute the SLOs, don't query 100 services on every refresh). (4) **The absence** (a service that *stops emitting* is "unknown," not "healthy" — a missing-data alert). "A health dashboard that shows 40 raw graphs is a *fire drill*; one that shows 'which SLOs are at risk' is a *decision tool*. Build for the decision."

**Q43. How do you do *security* as an SRE? (Where do reliability and security meet?)**
→ They meet at **the blast radius**: a security incident *is* an availability incident (a compromised node, a DDoS, a leaked credential that takes down a service). SRE's role: (1) **The incident path** (a security page goes through the *same* incident process — a commander, a comms, a mitigation; "contain first" = isolate the compromised node / rotate the credential / cut the traffic). (2) **The resilience to attack** (rate limiting = DDoS defense, least-privilege creds = a leak is smaller, *egress control* = data can't leave). (3) **The overlap in the SLO** (a *security* event that degrades the SLO burns the *same* budget — so "we're under attack" is a reliability number, not just a security one). (4) **The hard one**: *key rotation* and *cert renewal* are SRE toil (a cert expiring at 3am is a reliability incident) — automate them or they *will* page. "Security and reliability share the *blast radius* — the SRE's job is that a security event *degrades* the service, never *destroys* it, and that the *mundane* security (certs, keys) is *automated* so it never becomes an incident."

**Q44. You're designing on-call for a company going from 5 to 50 services. The page count is about to 10x. Plan.**
→ The 10x in *services* will be a 100x in *pages* if you don't change the *model*. (1) **Triage by SLO, not service** (page on the *user-facing SLO* breach, not on 50 individual service alerts — a cascade of 10 services is *one* page: "the checkout SLO is burning"). (2) **Group by *symptom*** (a DB down causes 10 service alerts → 1 page: "the payments DB is down"). (3) **Tier the services** (not all 50 deserve a *page* — tier-1 (user-critical) pages, tier-2 (tickets), tier-3 (dashboards only)). (4) **Scale the rotation** (50 services → more than one *team*/rotation; a service *owns* its on-call, the platform team owns the *rails*). (5) **The number**: set a *page budget* (e.g., < 10 pages/week/rotation) and *cut* to it (demote, consolidate, add context). "Going 5→50 services, the on-call *model* must change from 'a service pages' to 'an *SLO* pages' — otherwise you don't have 10x pages, you have 50 *rotations* that nobody can staff."

**Q45. Explain *why* a distributed system needs *idempotency* (this is asked a lot — make it concrete).**
→ Because the network *will* retry: a client times out (but the request *arrived*), a queue redelivers (at-least-once), a load balancer retries on a 502. Without idempotency, *retries create duplicates* (a double charge, a double shipment, a double notification). Concrete: a *payment* — the client sends `POST /charge` with an `Idempotency-Key`; the server stores (key → result); a retry with the same key returns the *stored* result, not a *new* charge. The *design* rule: **every write in a distributed system is idempotent** (upserts, conditional inserts, "if not already done"), because *at-least-once delivery is the default* (queues, retries, LBs all give it to you). "Idempotency isn't a nice-to-have — it's the *assumption* that at-least-once delivery is *safe*. Without it, every retry is a *corruption*."

**Q46. How do you design *multi-region active-active* for a stateful app (a shopping cart)?**
→ The cart is *stateful* (it has data) — active-active means the cart must be *readable/writable* in 2 regions with *fast failover*. The options: (1) **Per-user affinity** (a user's cart lives in *one* region — route by user-ID hash; failover = re-home the user to the other region, which needs the cart's *data* to have replicated). (2) **The replication** (the cart store must be *multi-region replicated* — a global KV like DynamoDB global tables, or a CRDT if it's a merge-y cart). (3) **The hard part: conflicts** (a user adds to the cart in both regions — a CRDT *merges*, a last-writer-wins *drops* one; say which and *why*). (4) **The SLO** (failover must be < the SLO's budget — DNS + routing + the data catch-up all in the minutes). "Active-active for *state* is the hardest SRE problem — the *state* is the constraint. The answer is usually 'per-entity affinity + a replicated store + a defined conflict rule,' and the *conflict rule* is the part you must *decide and document*, not discover in an incident."

## Part 4 — Scenario-Based (real-time, on-call)

> Use the 5-step framework: **Stabilize → Diagnose → Communicate → Fix → Learn.** Structure is half the grade.

**S1. 3am page: "checkout SLO burning — error budget 60% gone in 2h." You're the first responder. Go.**
→ **Declare + commander** (you're IC until handed off; open the incident channel). **Stabilize first**: is it a *deploy* in the last 30 min? If yes → **roll back** (fastest mitigation — don't debug a live burn). If no deploy → check the *denominator* (is a *dependency* down — the PSP, the DB? a regional issue?). **Communicate** (the channel + a status: "checkout degraded, investigating the payment path, next update 15 min"). **Diagnose** (the trace of a *failed* checkout — which hop? the error log for that cohort). **Fix** (rollback / fail the PSP over / scale — whatever stops the burn; *verify* the budget rate dropped, don't just "it looks better"). **Learn** (a postmortem: why did the budget burn 60% in 2h and did the *fast-burn alert* fire in time? was the rollback one-click?). "The 3am goal is *stop the burn* (rollback is the answer 80% of the time), *not* find the root cause — the cause is for the 9am postmortem, on a *healthy* system."

**S2. A deploy went out at 2pm. At 2:20, p99 went from 200ms to 2s. No errors. What do you do?**
→ **This is a *latency* regression, not an error one** (no errors = the old-style alert won't catch it — *that's the gap*). **Stabilize**: roll back (the 2pm deploy is the *correlation* — p99 bending right after a deploy is the deploy until proven otherwise; roll back, *then* investigate on a healthy system). **Verify** (p99 back to 200ms — confirm the *metric*, not "the page feels faster"). **Diagnose** (on the old version: *what* in the deploy added latency? a new *downstream call* (the #1), a *N+1 query*, a *synchronous* call that should be async, a *regex*, a *lock*). **The systemic fix**: a *latency* canary (the deploy's p99 is *gated*, not just error rate — a 2s p99 should have *aborted the canary*, not shipped to 100%). "No errors + slow p99 is the *most common* silent regression — and the fix is a *latency gate* on the canary, because 'it doesn't error' is not 'it's fast.'"

**S3. The database is at 95% CPU and climbing. The app is slowing. It's not a deploy. Go.**
→ **Stabilize**: (1) *shed load* (turn off non-critical features / queue the batch jobs that hit the DB) — reduce the *demand* while you diagnose. (2) *scale* if the DB can (a read replica for read-heavy, or a bigger instance — but a *CPU-bound* DB won't be saved by a replica if it's *write*-bound). **Diagnose**: the *query* — top queries by CPU/locks (the `pg_stat_statements` / `sys.dm` view). The usual suspects: a *runaway query* (a missing index on a table that *grew* — the query that was fast at 1M rows is slow at 10M), a *lock* (a long transaction holding a row), a *batch job* (the 2am job that's now running at 2pm). **Fix** (kill the runaway query, add the index — but an *index* on a 95%-CPU DB is *slow* to build, so *shed load first*, then index). **Learn** (a *query* alert: "a single query > X% of CPU" — the 95% DB was *one query*, and there was no alert on the *query*, only the *DB*). "A 95%-CPU DB is *usually one query* — the SRE move is to find the *query* (not 'the DB is slow'), and the *stabilize* is to *reduce demand* (shed/queue) while you find it, because a *bigger* DB is rarely the answer to a *bad* query."

**S4. You page on-call for "high error rate on service X." By the time you look, it's already resolved. Now what?**
→ **Don't close it as "it fixed itself"** — that's how you learn nothing and how the *next* one catches you. (1) **Reconstruct** (the metrics: *when* did it start/stop? what was the *shape* — a spike, a step? a *single* blip or *recurring*? the error log for that *window*). (2) **Correlate** (a deploy? a *cron job* (the 3am batch)? a *dependency's* blip? *traffic* (a spike that overloaded it)? a *cert* that expired and auto-renewed?). (3) **Classify** (was it *user-impacting* (the SLO burned) or *not* (a blip below the SLO)? — that decides if it's an *incident* (postmortem) or a *near-miss* (a ticket + a "why didn't we catch it earlier" note)). (4) **The systemic question**: if it *reoccurs*, will the *alert* catch it *faster*? (a *self-resolving* incident that takes 10 min to *notice* has a *detection* problem). (5) **Document** (even a 1-paragraph "what happened + what we'd do to detect it sooner" — a *resolved-before-you-looked* incident is a *gift*: no pressure, full data, and a chance to *improve detection* for free). "A self-resolved incident is *free practice* — the SRE who treats it as 'nothing happened' is *wasting the one incident they can study calmly*."

**S5. Two services are in a *dependency loop* (A calls B, B calls A) and it's causing a cascade (both are timing out). Go.**
→ **This is a *livelock*/cascading-failure** (each is waiting on the other, timeouts pile up, both saturate). **Stabilize**: **break the loop** — cut *one* direction (a feature flag on the A→B call, or a circuit breaker on *one* of the edges) so *one* service can respond without the other. You can't fix a loop by fixing *both* at once (they're *mutually* blocked) — you must *break* it. **Diagnose**: *why* is there a loop? (a *new* dependency added in a recent deploy that created the cycle — check the *last* change to A or B; or a *retry storm* — A retries B, B's retry calls A, the retries *amplify*). **Fix** (remove the *cycle* — one of the calls should be *async* (a queue, not a sync call) or *removed*; add a *circuit breaker* on both edges so a *future* loop fails *fast* instead of *cascading*). **Learn** (a *dependency graph* alert: "a new *cycle* was added" — a *static* check on the service graph that catches a loop *before* it ships, not in production). "A dependency *loop* is a *design* bug that *looks* like a *performance* bug — the SRE move is to *break one edge* (stabilize), find the *cycle* (diagnose), and add a *graph check* so the *next* loop is caught in *review*, not in *production*."

**S6. A *single* large customer is doing a *denial-of-service* on *themselves* (a bug in *their* integration is hammering your API 100x, and it's slowing *other* customers). Go.**
→ **This is a *noisy neighbor* + a *tenant isolation* failure.** **Stabilize**: **throttle *that* tenant** (a per-tenant rate limit / quota — you may need to *degrade a specific customer* to protect the *others*; this is a *business* call, so *page the account team* in parallel). **Diagnose**: confirm it's *their* traffic (the per-tenant metrics — 100x *their* RPS, *their* client-ID/IP range). **Fix**: the *immediate* (throttle them to their *agreed* quota), and the *structural* (**tenant isolation** — per-tenant *queues/pools/limits* so one tenant *cannot* starve the others; the fact that they *could* is the *design gap*). **The business angle**: *their* bug is *their* problem, but the *fact that it affected others* is *yours* — communicate to the *affected* customers ("a third party's traffic was degrading service; we've isolated it") and to *them* ("your integration is at 100x; here's the limit; here's the help"). **Learn** (a *per-tenant* SLO + a *noisy-neighbor* alert: "tenant X is > N% of total capacity"). "A noisy *tenant* is a *multi-tenancy* bug wearing a *performance* mask — the SRE fix is *isolation + quota* (so it *can't* happen), and the *business* fix is a *conversation* with the customer (so they *stop*)."

**S7. You're in an incident, and the *fix* you're about to apply (a config change) is *untested* and you're *not sure it'll work*. The service is still degraded. Go.**
→ **This is the *risk* moment.** The options: (1) **Try it** (the service is *already* degraded — an *untested* fix has *upside* (it works) and *downside* (it makes it worse). The key question: is the downside *reversible*? If *yes* (a config you can flip back in 1 min) → *try it, but* make it *reversible* (a flag, not a destructive change), *scope* it (1 node / 5% of traffic, not all of it), and *watch the metric* for 60s before declaring it worked). (2) **If it's *not* reversible** (a data change, a schema, a delete) → *don't* — a *bad* irreversible change at 3am is *worse* than a *degraded* service. Fall back to the *reversible* mitigation (rollback, shed load, failover) and *buy time* for a *tested* fix. (3) **Communicate the *decision*** ("I'm trying an *untested but reversible* config on 5% of traffic; if the metric doesn't improve in 60s I'll revert and we continue on the rollback path"). **The principle**: *at 3am, prefer the *reversible* and the *small*; the *irreversible* and the *big* wait for 9am and a *tested* change.* "The 3am rule: *reversible + small = try now; irreversible + big = wait.* The *service being degraded* doesn't justify an *irreversible* gamble — it justifies the *smallest reversible* step, and *communication* that you're choosing *deliberately*, not *desperately*."

**S8. Postmortem: the *root cause* is "a human made a mistake" (they typed the wrong command). But the *real* question is — how do you make the postmortem *blameless* AND *useful*?**
→ **Blameless ≠ "no cause."** The *human action* is a *symptom*, not the *root cause*. The *root cause* is the *system* that *allowed* a *one-typo* to *delete prod*: (1) **Why was the command *able* to target prod?** (no *confirmation*, no *dry-run*, no *scope limit* — the tool let a *global* `rm`/`drop` run). (2) **Why wasn't it *caught*?** (no *guardrail* — a policy that blocks destructive ops without a second approval; no *blast-radius limit*). (3) **Why was the *recovery* slow?** (no *backup* that was *tested*, no *rollback*). The *action items* are *systemic* (add a *dry-run* mode, require *2-person* approval for destructive ops, a *tested* backup, a *guardrail* policy) — **none of them are "be more careful"** (that's not *actionable* and it's not *blameless*). **The phrasing**: "an engineer ran a *broad* command; the *system* should have made the *narrow* command the *only* one that worked" — the *human* is *part of the system*, and the *fix* is to make the *safe* path the *easy* path. "A postmortem that ends at 'human error' is *done early* — the *blameless* move is to ask '*what in the system made the mistake possible and the recovery slow*,' and to write *action items* that *remove the possibility*, not the *person's* carelessness."

**S9. Your *error budget* is *spent* (0 left) for the month, but the *business* has a *launch* in 3 days that *needs* a *risky* deploy. What do you do?**
→ **This is the SRE's *hardest* call — and it's a *business* decision, not a *technical* one.** (1) **Make the *risk* *legible*** (the budget is 0 → *any* failure *breaches* the SLO; quantify: "this deploy has a ~X% chance of a *N-minute* degradation; the *cost* is *Y* in the SLO"). (2) **Offer the *options*, not a *no***: (a) **Ship *now*, *accept* the risk** (the *business* owns it — *documented*, with the *number*); (b) **Ship *partially*** (a *canary at 5%* + a *feature flag* so the *risky* part is *off* for 90% of users — get the *launch value* with a *smaller* blast radius); (c) **Delay *N* days** (wait for the budget to *refill* / a *safer* window — is a *3-day* delay acceptable for the *launch*?). (3) **If they choose (a)**: *harden* it (a *canary* with an *auto-rollback* on *any* SLO burn, a *war room* for the launch, the *rollback* tested *before* the deploy, a *status page* ready). (4) **The *principle***: the SRE *doesn't* *veto* the launch — we make the *price* in *reliability* *explicit*, offer the *cheaper* options, and *document* the *decision* (so if it *breaks*, it's a *known, accepted* risk, not a *surprise*). "The error budget *spent* means the *next* failure is a *breach* — the SRE's job is to make the *business* *see* that number, offer the *smaller-blast-radius* path, and *record* the *choice*. We *informed* the decision; we didn't *block* it. That's the *difference* between an *adversary* and a *partner*."

**S10. You're asked to *design the SRE practice* for a startup that has *no* SLOs, *no* on-call, and *3* engineers (who are also the devs). How do you start?**
→ **You don't hire an SRE team — you *add SRE *habits* to the 3 engineers.** (1) **Week 1: the *one* SLO** (pick the *single* user-critical SLI — "a successful *checkout* in < 2s" — baseline it, set *one* SLO, *one* fast-burn alert. *One* is enough to start; 10 SLOs is *paralysis*). (2) **Week 2: the *page* discipline** (a *rotation* (2 of the 3, 1 week, with a *follow* week off), a *runbook* for the *one* alert, and the *rule* "a *page* is only for the *SLO* — everything else is a *ticket*"). (3) **Week 3: the *postmortem* habit** (the *first* incident, even a small one, gets a *written* postmortem with *action items* — the *habit* matters more than the *depth*). (4) **Month 2: the *levers*** (a *rollback* that's *one-click* (the #1 MTTR reducer), a *canary* (so a *bad* deploy *auto-stops*), *toil* identified (the *one* manual thing that eats hours → *automate* it)). (5) **The *mindset***: SRE at 3 engineers is *10% of the time* on *reliability* (the *SLO*, the *on-call*, the *postmortem*) and *90%* on *features* — the *goal* is *not* to *become* SREs, it's to make the *3 engineers* *reliable by default*. "A startup can't *afford* an SRE *team* — it *can* afford an SRE *discipline*. The *sequence* is *one SLO → one page → one postmortem → one-click rollback*, because each is *small*, each *compounds*, and *together* they turn '3 devs who *hope* it's up' into '3 devs who *know* it's up.'"

## Part 5 — Questions to ask the interviewers

- "What's your current error budget spend, and what does the team *do* differently when it's below 20%?"
- "Walk me through your *last* page — from the alert to the mitigation, how many minutes, and what was the *first* thing on-call did?"
- "What's the *one* alert you'd delete tomorrow, and why did it survive so long?"
- "How do you decide a change is *risky enough* to need a canary vs. just ship it?"
- "What's the *most recent* postmortem action item that's *still open*, and why?" (reveals whether postmortems *actually* close)
